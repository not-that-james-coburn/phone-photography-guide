A beginner's picture book

You can take
better photos.

Your phone already has the camera. This little book will help you see, frame, and capture with confidence.

No fancy gear required.

01 / The big idea

Photography is mostly about seeing.

A better camera can help. But your biggest upgrade is learning to notice light, subject, background, and timing.

MOVECan I change my angle?

Think less about “taking a picture” and more about making a picture.
02 / Start clean

Clean the lens.

It sounds boring. It makes a surprisingly big difference.

  • Wipe the camera lens with a soft cloth.
  • Check for fingerprints before important shots.
  • Keep your phone case from covering the lens.
03 / Composition

Give your subject a place to live.

Turn on your camera's grid. Put important subjects near the intersections instead of automatically centering everything.

Rule of thirds: a useful starting point, not a law. Centering can be great when the scene is symmetrical.
04 / Light

Find the good light.

Try the light50%

Move the slider to see how changing light changes contrast.

When a photo looks flat or harsh, move the subject before changing camera settings.

05 / Exposure

Too bright? Too dark? You are in charge.

Tap the part of the scene you care about. On many phones, you can then drag the brightness control up or down.

☀
− darkerbrighter +
A slightly darker photo can preserve bright sky detail. Don't be afraid to bring exposure down.
06 / Focus

Tell the phone what matters.

Tap your subject to focus there. For a moving subject, tap again as needed.

Many phones also let you press and hold to lock focus/exposure. The exact gesture varies by camera app.
07 / Zoom

When you can, move your feet.

At 1×, the sign is part of the scene. Try moving closer instead of enlarging the same pixels.

Digital zoom can throw away detail. If it is safe and practical, change your position first. If your phone has a real telephoto lens, its optical zoom can be useful.

08 / Angles

One subject. Five photos.

Before you leave, make yourself move.

eye levellowhighclosefar
Challenge: take five versions of the same subject. Pick your favorite afterward.
09 / Leading lines

Let lines point the way.

Roads, fences, railings, shadows, walls, and rows of flowers can lead the viewer's eye toward your subject.

10 / Backgrounds

Look behind your subject.

A great subject can disappear against a cluttered background. Take one step left, right, up, or down to simplify it.

11 / Portraits

Portraits love soft light.

  • Try open shade instead of direct overhead sun.
  • Give your subject room to look or move into.
  • Watch for distracting objects “growing” from their head.
12 / Food & objects

Get closer. Simplify. Try a new angle.

For small subjects, fill more of the frame. Try shooting from above for a flat-lay look, or lower for depth and drama.

13 / Action

Take more frames.

1234 ✓56

For pets, kids, sports, and candid moments, use burst or rapid shooting if your phone offers it. Choose the sharpest moment afterward.

Don't judge the photo while you're still trying to catch the moment.
14 / Low light

Steady wins.

In low light, tiny movements become blur. Brace your phone against a wall, table, railing, or other stable surface when possible.

Night mode can help. Hold still until the capture finishes.
15 / Reflections

Look for mirrors in unexpected places.

Puddles, windows, polished floors, mirrors, and calm water can create symmetry and a second world inside your frame.

16 / Negative space

Empty space is not empty.

Leaving room around a subject can make it feel calm, important, lonely, funny, or tiny.

Try it: place one small subject against a large, simple area.
